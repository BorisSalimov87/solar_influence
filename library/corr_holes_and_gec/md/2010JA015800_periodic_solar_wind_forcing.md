Introduction** Understanding the complex interplay between the solar variability and space 32 weather is very important for forecasting the disturbances in the Geospace environment 33 that can affect the astronauts in space, payloads on board the satellites, space based 34 communication and navigational systems as well as ground based power grid systems. 35 The Coronal Mass Ejections (CMEs) and solar flares are the sources of high energy 36 particles and are of primary importance because of their huge and immediate impact on 37 the Earth. Further, the Coronal Holes (CHs) on the Sun, the principal sources of high 38 speed solar wind particles, are another important source of geomagnetic disturbances on 39 the Earth. The Coronal Holes are mainly unipolar magnetic field regions on the Sun with 40 one foot print of field lines are attached to Sun and other are extending in to the 41 interplanetary space giving rise to emission of high-speed solar wind streams [Krieger et 42 al., 1973]. The proton density in CHs is typically 2 – 3 times lower than that of the quiet 43 5 6 Sun and the temperature is around 10 - 10 K [Munro and Withbroe, 1972]. Due to their 44 low density and emission measure, CHs can be identified as dark areas on X-ray and 45 EUV images of the Sun and dominant unipolar regions on magnetograms [Reeves and 46 Parkinson, 1970; Wiegelmann and Solanki, 2004]. When the interplanetary magnetic 47

field is oriented southward, strong coupling between the magnetized plasma within the 48 high speed streams and Earth’s magnetosphere results in transfer of mass, momentum 49 and energy producing geomagnetic disturbances. Since the CHs rotate almost rigidly 50 with the Sun, they produce recurrent high speed streams and geomagnetic disturbances in 51 the Geospace environment [Timothy et al. 1975 and Tsurutani et al. 1995]. 52 The coronal holes and solar wind streams undergo systematic variations over the 53 54 11-year solar cycle. Through much of the cycle, the Sun’s polar region is dominated by coronal holes, the sources of large, persistent and fast solar wind streams with an average 55 bulk speed of 500 – 800 km/s [Philips et al., 1995]. During the solar minimum, the polar 56 holes became large and extend into equatorial and low latitude regions with some tongue-57 like extensions [Krieger et al., 1973; Sheeley and Harvey, 1981]. In addition to fast 58 streams from polar holes, the solar wind has also dense low-speed streams (300 – 400 59 km/s) from the closed field regions near the equator, known as equatorial coronal 60 streamer belt. As the streams travel in the interplanetary space, the high-speed streams 61 catch the slow-speed streams and create regions of enhanced density and magnetic field, 62 called co-rotating interaction regions (CIRs) [Tsurutani et al. 1995]. When the coronal 63 high speed wind interacts with the preceding slow solar wind, forward and reverse shocks 64 often form at the CIR boundaries. The CIR interfaces can be characterized by a relatively 65 sharp density drop, temperature rise and a sharp increase in the solar wind bulk velocity. 66 CIRs are especially prominent during the declining phase of the solar cycle, when the 67 heliospheric magnetic field has a well-developed sector structure and coronal holes may 68 extend to low latitudes [Richardson 2004]. 69

The fast streams from the CHs and CIR interfaces have a tendency to repeat for 70 several solar rotations producing recurrent geomagnetic activity at 27-day periodicity. 71 However, recent investigations by Temmer et al. 2007 and Gibson et al. 2009 have 72 elucidated that the solar wind velocity and geomagnetic properties also exhibit sub-73 harmonic solar rotational periodicities at 13.5-day, 9-day and even shorter periods during 74 the descending and minimum phase of solar cycle 23. In response, the Earth's upper 75 atmospheric properties such as thermospheric density and composition, plasma 76 temperature, and electron density are also found to oscillate with the same periodicities 77 [Lei et al., 2008a; Crowley et al., 2008; Sojka et al., 2009; Tulasi Ram et al., 2010]. 78 The electron density in the ionosphere responds significantly to the changes in the 79 thermosphere composition, neutral density, temperature, neutral winds as well as the 80 electric fields, which in turn greatly varies with latitude, altitude, local time, season and 81 the phase of the geomagnetic activity. Because of various neutral and electro-dynamic 82 coupling processes are involved, the global ionospheric response to geomagnetic storms 83 is much complex and been the subject of extensive investigations by both case studies as 84 well as model simulations during the past 3-4 decades [Prolss, 1980; Fuller-Rowell et al., 85 1994, 1996; Buansanto 1999; Mendillo 2006 and the references therein]. However, the 86 earlier studies on the altitudinal response of global ionosphere to geomagnetic storms 87 were mostly based on the model simulations due to lack of global observations of vertical 88 electron density profiles. Although the amplitude is smaller, the solar wind interfaces 89 produce ionospheric changes similar to those associated with isolated geomagnetic 90 storms [Mendillo and Schatter, 1983]. Hence, the short-period recurrent geomagnetic 91 disturbances associated with periodic solar wind forcing during the low solar activity 92

periods provide an opportunity for a comprehensive investigation on the global scale 93 ionospheric response to minor geomagnetic storms. 94 Therefore, the objective of the present investigation is for further understanding of 95 96 periodic solar wind streams from recurrent coronal holes and its impact on Earth’s geomagnetic and ionospheric properties. In this context, we first present the results of a 97 systematic study of periodicities in the solar wind velocity and the area of coronal hole 98 regions during the complete period of solar cycle 23. Secondly, we focus our 99 investigation on extremely low solar activity period of 2008 to study the modulation of 100 interplanetary solar wind parameters due to recurrent coronal holes and its imprints on 101 the Earth’s geomagnetic and ionospheric properties. Further, we present the seasonal 102 dependence on the ionospheric response to recurrent geomagnetic activity associated with 103 periodic solar wind forcing at different latitudinal-altitudinal regions. 104

207 **2.2 Periodic modulation of interplanetary SW parameters and their imprints on** 208 **Earth’s geomagnetic activity during extreme solar minimum period, 2008.** We now focus our attention on the sub-harmonic periodicity of 9-day period as a 209 diagnostic tool to study the periodic modulation in the interplanetary solar wind 210 properties and their imprints on Earth’s geomagnetic properties. The reasons for 211 choosing the 9-day periodicity are explained in the next section. With a view to 212 investigate the 9-day periodicity in the fractional CH area and its modulation on the 213 interplanetary solar wind properties, the ACE’s high resolution (64-second) Level-2 data 214 during the solar minimum period in 2008 are carefully examined. This period was 215 selected because the wavelet of both CH area and SW velocity exhibits strong 9-day 216 periodicity during most of the year 2008 and also because of the availability of Formosat-217 3/COSMIC vertical electron density profile data to further study the seasonal dependence 218 on latitudinal and altitudinal response of ionospheric electron density to this 9-day 219 periodicity. 220 Figures 4(a) to (h) shows the fractional CH area derived from STEREO-EUVI 221 images along with SW bulk velocity, SW magnetic field magnitude, |B|, SW proton 222 temperature, SW proton density, vertical component, IMF Bz, symmetrical ring current 223 index, SymH and geomagnetic activity index, Kp, respectively, during 2008. Although 224 the 9-day periodicity in SW velocity can be seen almost throughout the year 2008, the 225 data pertaining to the day numbers 1 to 120 are only shown in figure 4 for better 226 visibility. It can be seen from this figure that the variation of SW velocity corresponds 227 well with the variation of CH area with a phase delay of 2-3 days. This phase delay 228 generally agrees with the travelling time of SW particles from the source to orbit of ACE 229

at L1-point. A careful examination of figures 4(b) to 4(e) reveals that the interplanetary 230 SW properties exhibit the signatures of a series of co-rotating interaction regions (CIRs) 231 during this period. It can be observed from these figures that, every sharp increase in SW 232 velocity corresponds with a sharp increase in magnetic field magnitude, sharp increase in 233 SW proton temperature and a sharp decrease in SW proton density, which are the 234 signatures of CIR stream interface [Richardson et al. 1996, 1998, Tsurutani et al. 2006, 235 2008, Choi et al. 2009]. Further, the impulse type increase-and-decrease of magnetic 236 field magnitude, proton density preceding-and-after the increase of SW velocity indicates 237 the signatures of forward and reverse shocks associated with CIRs [Kennel et al. 1985, 238 Tsurutani et al. 2006, 2008 and Choi et al. 2009]. Lei et al. [2010] have made a detailed 239 investigation on the interplanetary solar wind parameters during 2008 and identified the 240 presence of 38 CIRs in the entire year 2008 using the method described in McPherron et 241 al. [2009]. Thus, the results from the figures 4(a)-(e) and the results from Lei et al. 242 [2010] indicate that a series of CIRs are formed at quasi 9-day periodicity because of the 243 interaction of fast solar wind stream trajectories from the equatorward extended or low-244 latitude (probably isolated) coronal holes with the preceding ambient slow solar wind 245 streams. The resultant CIR structures of enhanced density and magnetic field regions 246 impinge upon the Earth’s magnetosphere and can trigger recurrent geomagnetic activity 247 at quasi 9-day periodicity. For instance, from Figs. 4(g), it can be observed that a series 248 of recurrent geomagnetic storms of weak to moderate intensities (SymH -30 to -50 nT) 249 occurred throughout this period. A good correlation between the large positive and 250 negative excursions of IMF Bz and the corresponding decrease in SymH index as well as 251 simultaneous increase in Kp-index can be observed from figures 4(f), 4(g) and 4(h). 252

Because of highly oscillatory nature of Bz component with in CIRs [Fig. 4(f)], the 253 resultant geomagnetic storms are typically only weak to moderate intensity. However, 254 the recovery phases of these storms are elongated for several days or more as can be seen 255 from Fig. 4(g). 256 The Lomb-Scargle (LS) amplitude spectra [Lomb, 1976; Scargle, 1982] of 257 various parameters that described previously such as CH area, SW velocity, SW magnetic 258 field magnitude (|B|), IMF Bz, SymH index, Kp-Index during the entire year of 2008 are 259 presented in Figs. 5(a) to (f) respectively. Further, the LS spectra of Total Solar 260 Irradiance (TSI) and EUV irradiance at 5-105 nm wavelengths are also shown for 261 comparison in Figs. 5(g) and 5(h), respectively. Here, the TSI data is obtained from the 262 measurements of TIM instrument on board the SORCE satellite and EUV irradiance data 263 is obtained from the level-3 data products of Solar EUV Experiment (SEE) on board the 264 TIMED satellite. It can be clearly observed from this figure that the CH area, solar wind 265 parameters (SW velocity and SW magnetic field) as well as the geomagnetic activity 266 indices (SymH and Kp indices) exhibit pronounced spectral peaks at 13.5 and 9-day 267 periods. Further, the 9-day periodicity is strongly present in SW total magnetic field |B| 268 due to compression effects at the beginning of the CIRs. However, the 9-day periodicity 269 is absent in the vertical component Bz. This could be due to the large positive and 270 negative fluctuations in IMF Bz [Fig. 4(f)] at stream-stream compression regions, which 271 are the typical signatures of CIRs as described by Tsurutani 2005, 2006 and references 272 therein. On the other hand, the Total Solar Irradiance (TSI) and EUV irradiance do not 273 exhibit any significant spectral peaks at 13.5 and 9-day periods [Figs. 5(g) and 5(h)]. 274 Thus, the results presented in Figs. 4 and 5 clearly indicate that these sub-harmonic solar 275

rotational periodicities at 13.5 and 9-day periods in geomagnetic activity indices (SymH 276 and Kp) are primarily due to solar wind energy input associated with fast solar wind 277 streams from the recurrent coronal holes. The solar wind high-speed streams (HSS) 278 contain large-amplitude non-linear Alfven waves within the CIRs [Tsurutani et al. 1994, 279 Balogh et al. 1995]. The negative Bz components of the Alfven waves within the high-280 speed streams lead to magnetic reconnection and transfer of energy from solar wind to 281 the magnetosphere [Tsurutani and Gonzalez, 1987; Tsurutani et al. 2006], which is 282 characterized by continuous auroral activity called as “High-Intensity Long Duration 283 Continuous AE Activity (HILDCAA)”. The magnetic reconnection associated with the 284 Alfven waves cause continuous, shallow injections of solar wind plasma into the 285 magnetosphere at auroral and mid latitudes. Further, the Kp-index which is a useful 286 parameter to represent the particle precipitation at auroral and mid latitudes [Emery et al. 287 2008 and Emery et al. 2009] also exhibit coherent enhancements with SW velocity [Figs. 288 4(b) and 4(h)]. Therefore, the results from Figs. 4 and 5 clearly suggests that the fast 289 solar wind trajectories from CHs, enhanced magnetic field associated with CIRs and 290 Alfven wave associated magnetic field fluctuations in HSS-CIR structures trigger 291 recurrent geomagnetic activity of weak to moderate levels in the geo-space environment. 292 During the ascending phase and solar maximum, the high speed streams narrow 293 294 and weaken because of poleward shrinkage of coronal holes (for example, as can be seen from Fig. 2(a)). Additionally, the ambient solar wind flow is frequently perturbed by 295 energetic events on the Sun such as coronal mass ejections (CME) and solar flares. 296 Hence, the CME-associated geomagnetic storms can occur more frequently near the solar 297 maximum. Whereas the occurrence rate of HSS-CIR associated recurrent geomagnetic 298

storms maximizes during the declining phase and solar minimum [Tsurutani et al. 2006] 299 due to the equatorward extended and/or low latitude isolated coronal holes as can be 300 observed in Fig. 2(b). During the solar minimum in 2008, the Sun was exceptionally 301 quiet with more than 70% of spotless days (annual average sunspot number was only 3) 302 and global weakness in the heliospheric magnetic field [Gibson et al. 2009]. However, 303 the large areas of low-mid latitude CHs and high speed streams [Figs. 4(a) and 4(b)] are 304 persistent for several solar rotations resulting in recurrent HSS-CIR associated 305 geomagnetic storms during 2008 [Fig. 4(g) and 4(h)]. It can be noticed that the latitudinal variation of electron density perturbations and their 424 phase relationship with Kp at 450 km is quite different from that at 300 km. At 450 km 425 altitude [Fig. 8], the electron density perturbations are in-phase with the perturbations in 426 Kp and the negative phase relationship is mostly confined to high latitudes of summer 427 hemisphere. During the equinoxes [Figs. 8(a) and 8(b)], the density perturbations exhibit 428 in-phase relationship with Kp almost at all latitudes. These results indicate that the 429 ionospheric electron density response to 9-day quasi periodic geomagnetic activity is not 430 globally symmetric unlike the thermospheric mass density response as shown by Lei et 431 al. [2008b] and Thayer et al. [2008]. Instead, the ionospheric response exhibits 432 significant variation with latitude, altitude, local time as well as with season. 433 It was known that the upwelling neutral winds during geomagnetic storms carries 434 molecular-rich air across the pressure level to higher altitudes leading to the reduction in 435 O/N₂ density ratio at high latitudes [Fuller-Rowell et al. 1994]. Also, the downwelling 436

wind transports atomic-oxygen-rich air parcels to low altitudes and enhances the O/N₂ 437 density ratio at low latitudes [Strickland et al. 2001]. Thus, the out-of-phase (negative) 438 response at high latitudes and in-phase (positive) response at low latitudes observed at 439 300 km [Fig. 7] can be attributed as due to changes in the storm time composition 440 disturbances due to upwelling/downwelling at high/low latitudes [Crowley et al. 2008]. 441 Further, the results from Figs. 7 and 8 indicates that the region of disturbed composition 442 zone (negative phase response) penetrates deep into lower latitudes at 300 km (around 443 F2-layer peak altitudes) and limits to higher latitudes at 450 km (topside ionosphere). 444 Also, the latitudinal extent of composition disturbance zone is found to vary with local 445 time as well as with season [Figs. 7 and 8]. 446 In order to examine up to what latitudinal and altitudinal extent that the 447 composition changes may affect during different seasons and local times, we proceed 448 with the cross correlation analysis. The bandpass filtered 9-day periodic perturbations in 449 electron density at each latitude-altitude bin are cross correlated with the perturbations in 450 Kp and the zero-lag correlation coefficient is plotted as a function of latitude and altitude 451 during the respective seasons and local times as shown in Fig. 9. The positive correlation 452 coefficient indicate the enhancements in the electron density coherently with the 453 enhancements in the geomagnetic activity (positive response) and the negative correlation 454 indicate the reduction in electron density in response to enhanced geomagnetic activity 455 (negative response). It can be seen from this figure that the correlation coefficient in the 456 topside ionosphere (above 350 km) is positive except at high latitudes of northern 457 summer hemisphere. The positive response in the topside ionosphere can be attributed to 458 the thermal expansion of upper atmosphere and subsequent increase in the scale height 459

resulting from the enhanced plasma temperatures [Lei et al. 2008c, Sojka et al. 2009 and 460 Tulasi Ram et al. 2010]. Further, it can be noticed from Fig. 9 is that the latitudinal and 461 altitudinal extent of negative phase response due to disturbed neutral composition shows 462 significant seasonal and local time dependence. One prominent feature that can be 463 observed from Figs. 9(c)-(f) is that the region of negative correlation extends in to low 464 and equatorial latitudes in the summer hemisphere during both the day and night times. 465 Where as in the winter hemisphere, the negative correlation region is limited to high 466 o latitudes (> ~60 ) during the day [Figs. 9(c) and 9(e)] and confined only to polar region 467 during the night times [Figs. 9(d) and 9(f)]. These results clearly suggests that the 468 disturbed composition changes due to upwelling winds at high latitudes penetrates deeper 469 into low and equatorial latitudes in the summer hemisphere than in the winter 470 hemisphere. 471 When the Joule and particle heating occur at high latitudes during the 472 geomagnetic activity associated with recurrent high speed solar wind forcing, the heated 473 thermosphere expands and leads to an increase in the neutral scale height (kT/mg). 474 While the larger effect is at high latitude, the atmospheric pressure tends to equilibrate 475 globally, the corresponding expansion occurs at all latitudes. Recently, Sojka et al [2009] 476 have shown that the ion temperature measured at 300 km shows strong enhancements 477 associated with CIRs in high speed solar wind streams. Plasma temperature changes 478 would cause similar changes in the plasma scale height, which is a measure of thickness 479 of the ionosphere. The increase in the scale height due to thermal expansion associated 480 with plasma temperature will cause similar increase in the F2-layer peak altitudes (hmF2). 481 For example, Tulasi Ram et al. [2010] have also shown the 9-day quasi periodic 482

enhancements in the daytime topside ionospheric thickness (HT) and the F2-layer peak 483 altitude (hmF2) coherently with the geomagnetic activity (Kp) at all latitudes. Therefore, 484 when one measures the electron density at any fixed height in the topside ionosphere, the 485 increase/decrease in the scale height and hmF2 causes corresponding increase/decrease of 486 electron density. Thus, the observed positive response in the topside ionosphere (above 487 350 km altitude) in Fig. 9 is related to corresponding changes in the scale height and 488 hmF2 due to thermal expansion associated with plasma temperature. Further, the 489 equatorward wind during enhanced geomagnetic activity will also raise the F-layer to 490 higher altitudes. This would also contribute to the coherent enhancements in the hmF2 as 491 observed by Tulasi Ram et al. [2010]. However, the upwelling wind during enhanced 492 geomagnetic activity carries the molecular rich air to higher altitudes at the same time 493 leading to a decrease in O/N₂ ratio. The electron density near F2-layer peak altitudes is 494 mostly determined by the balance of production due to photoionization of O and loss due 495 to N₂ [Rishbeth and Garriott, 1969]. Thus, the electron density at F2 peak altitudes 496 responds to changes in neutral composition (O/N₂ density ratio) brought about by the 497 upwelling/downwelling winds at high/low latitudes during the enhanced geomagnetic 498 activity. The dominant process driving the electron density changes in the night time 499 ionosphere is the loss through recombination. Thus, the changes in N₂ and O₂ 500 concentrations due to upwelling will modify the chemical loss rates and in turn electron 501 densities. Therefore, the electron density response around 200 to 350 km is dominated by 502 the changes in neutral composition brought about by the global thermospheric circulation. 503 Further, there will be a mean transequatorial neutral wind from the summer 504 hemisphere to winter hemisphere during the solstices. Also, the summer-to-winter 505

transequatorial neutral wind maximizes during the night due to reduced ion-drag. The 506 summer-to-winter hemispheric wind will be in same direction to the equatorward wind in 507 the summer hemisphere. On the other hand, the summer-to-winter hemispheric wind is 508 in the opposite direction to the equatorward wind in the winter hemisphere. Thus, the 509 latitudinal and altitudinal extent of disturbed composition changes observed in Fig. 9 510 depends on the relative strengths and directions of the summer-to-winter transequatorial 511 wind and the equatorward wind due to high latitude heating during the periods of 512 enhanced geomagnetic activity [Buansanto 1999]. In the summer hemisphere, the total 513 wind is augmented by the prevailing summer-to-winter transequatorial wind and thus 514 causes the composition disturbance zone to extend deep into low and equatorial latitudes 515 as may be seen from Figs. 9(c)-(f). However, the summer-to-winter wind impedes the 516 equatorward motion of the composition changes in the winter hemisphere. Thus, the 517 o 518 negative phase response in the winter hemisphere is limited to high latitudes (~ 60 ) during the day time [Figs. 9(c) and 9(e)]. Further, the disturbed composition zone is 519 limited only to polar region during the night time [Figs. 9(d) and 9(f)]. This could be 520 possibly due to large oppositely acting transequatorial summer-to-winter winds during 521 the night times. 522 523 **3. Summary** A comprehensive investigation on the solar rotational and its sub-harmonic 524 periodicities in coronal hole regions and solar wind velocity has been carried out, for the 525 first time, using the solar EUV image data from the complete solar cycle 23 and its 526 impact on the Earth’s geomagnetic and ionospheric electron density have been studied 527

during the extreme solar minimum period, 2008. The important observations and results 528 are herein summarized in the following paragraphs. 529 Both the solar wind velocity [Fig. 1(a)] and fractional CH area [Fig. 3(a)] 530 consistently exhibit similar features with large values during the declining phase and the 531 minimum of SC 23 indicating that the equatorward extension of polar holes and low-532 latitude (probably isolated) holes are prevailing during the declining phase of SC 23 (i.e., 533 from 2003-2009). Further, both the SW velocity as well as the CH area exhibit greater 534 tendency for sub-harmonic (13.5-day and 9-day) periodicities during the declining phase 535 and minimum of SC 23. This suggests two/three large CH regions that longitudinally 536 o o distributed by 180 /120 apart and persistent for several rotations may produce sub-537 harmonic periodicities in the fractional CH area. This feature is more prominent during 538 the declining phase and solar minimum because of the equatorward extension of polar 539 holes or low-latitude isolated holes. Even though the previous solar minimum (1996-540

1998) period has the nearly the same solar activity (F10.7 solar flux) levels as the 541 minimum of SC 23 (2005-2009), the values of both CH area and SW velocity are smaller 542 during the previous solar minimum and the sub-harmonic periodicities are only 543 occasionally observed for short periods [Figs. 1 and 3]. This speculates that the 544 frequently observed, long lasting (several solar rotations) sub-harmonic periodicities in 545 CH area and SW properties during the declining phase from 2003 to 2009 [Figs. 1(b) and 546 3(b)] may probably be a unique feature of SC 23. The recurrent solar wind high speed 547 streams (HSS) from the several rotations of equatorward extended or low-latitude 548 (probably isolated) coronal holes interact with the preceding ambient slow solar wind 549 stream and produce recurrent co-rotating interaction regions (CIRs). The resultant HSS-550

CIR structures of enhanced density and magnetic field regions recurrently impinge upon 551 the Earth’s magnetosphere. The magnetic reconnection associated with the Alfven waves 552 in the HSS-CIR structures cause continuous, shallow injections of solar wind plasma into 553 554 the magnetosphere and trigger recurrent geomagnetic activity of weak to moderate levels in the geo-space environment [Fig. 4]. Further, the LS amplitude spectra [Fig. 5] of CH 555 area, SW parameters and geomagnetic activity indices (SymH and Kp) show similar 556 features with strong spectral peaks at 13.5 and 9-day periods. The results from Figs. 4 557 and 5 clearly suggests that the sub-harmonic solar rotational periodicities in the 558 geomagnetic activity (SymH and Kp indices) are primarily due to solar wind energy input 559 associated with CIR structures within the fast solar wind streams from the recurrent 560 coronal holes. 561 The ionospheric electron density response to this HSS-CIR induced recurrent 562 geomagnetic activity has been investigated using the Formosat-3/COSMIC radio 563 occultation measurements during the extremely low solar activity year 2008. From Fig.7, 564 it was elucidated that the both day and night side electron densities exhibit periodic 565 oscillations at solar rotational (27-day) and its sub-harmonic periodicities (13.5 and 9-566 day) in the electron density. Further, the spectral amplitudes of these oscillations in 567 ionospheric electron density correlate well with the oscillations in the geomagnetic 568 activity index, Kp. The important feature of 9-day periodicity in electron density and Kp 569 is considered as a diagnostic tool to investigate the global ionospheric response to HSS-570 CIR associated recurrent geomagnetic activity. In general, the topside ionospheric 571 response (above 350 km) appears to be dominated by the changes in the plasma 572 temperature and/or scale height and exhibits coherent enhancements with the 573

perturbations in the Kp during both the day and night times. However, the electron 574 density response between 200 and 350 km altitudes is dominated by the changes in the 575 neutral composition and exhibits significant latitudinal, local time and seasonal 576 variations. The upwelling winds at high latitudes significantly alters the neutral 577 composition (O/N₂ density ratio) and results in a decrease in electron density during the 578 enhanced geomagnetic activity. In the summer hemisphere, the composition disturbance 579 zone penetrates deep into low and equatorial latitudes during both day and night times 580 where the total wind is augmented by the summer-to-winter transequatorial wind. In the 581 winter hemisphere, the summer-to-winter wind is in the opposite direction and limits the 582 o composition disturbance zone to high latitudes (> ~60 ) during the day time. Further, the 583 enhanced transequatorial summer-to-winter wind during the night time restricts the 584 composition disturbance zone only to polar region of winter hemisphere. 585 The recurrent geomagnetic disturbances associated with co-rotating interaction 586 regions (CIR) in the solar wind high speed streams during the extreme solar minimum 587 period, 2008 facilitates an opportunity to investigate the diurnal and seasonal dependence 588 of the ionospheric response at different latitude and altitude regions. The results obtained 589 from this investigation can be applicable to the global ionospheric response to minor 590 (isolated) geomagnetic storms and thus provide some important insights to future 591 modeling works on the ionosphere-thermosphere response to geomagnetic storms and 592 solar wind-magnetosphere-ionosphere-thermosphere coupling processes. 593
